Where values come from

Every metadata value in the catalog carries its evidence: the source it was read from, a locator and the quoted text. This page counts, for release R2026.43, the 3,165,350 metadata values the catalog holds about its samples, by the source each came from, so you can see what each kind of source adds.

How to read these numbers

Values, not columns. Methods counts one value per sample and column, so its totals are larger. Related columns form one concept and count once: country, region, locality, site and latitude / longitude are one location; a coded value and its free-text detail (e.g. health condition + diagnosis text) are one value; gestational age and preterm status are one prematurity value. A concept counts once per sample and source — a sample whose country comes from the archive and whose recruitment hospital comes from the paper has one location value from each. Precedence. Each sample has at most one current value per column; when several sources give it, the archive record wins over the supplementary table, then the paper text, then the abstract, so the counts show what each source adds to the shipped table. Varies by sample is the share of values in project × concept combinations where the source gives more than one distinct value; the rest are single statements for a whole project, such as "all participants were recruited in Kenya" or "no antibiotics in the 3 months before sampling". The birth and feeding fields are counted the same way; 'unknown' values are not counted.

Longitudinal sampling and relatedness: how they are derived

People. 390,368 samples carry a person identifier read from a source (BioSample attributes such as host_subject_id, sample names decoded by two agreeing rules, participant columns of supplementary tables). 106,560 more carry a generated identifier (the sample accession, marked generated on the sample page) because the paper or archive states that every sequenced sample comes from a different person — for example "a single stool sample was collected from each participant", or a stated number of participants equal to the number of samples with no mention of repeated sampling. Two independent model readings must agree, and it is never done for longitudinal, before–after, multi-visit, replicate or pooled designs.

Longitudinal projects (1,380, with 66,790 people sampled more than once and 277,176 samples; plus 413 projects whose source describes repeated sampling but whose samples cannot yet be assigned to people). A person's samples are ordered on one time axis, chosen in this order: age at collection (when every sample has it and it varies), full collection dates, then timepoint labels converted to days ("week 4" = 28 d, "month 6" = 183 d, "baseline" and "birth" = 0). From these the project page shows the number of people sampled repeatedly, samples per person, follow-up length (first to last sample per person; median and maximum) and the median interval between consecutive samples, with a timeline of up to 36 people. The sampling schedule and time origin quoted from the paper are shown alongside.

The study-design block at the top of every project page is assembled from catalog values; no extra model reading is involved. The design type comes, in this order, from the reading of the paper, from two independent readings of the paper, abstract or archive record that agreed, from the classification made when the project was admitted (“two readings differed” when its two readings disagreed), or from the sample variables themselves (intervention arms, cases and healthy controls, repeated sampling; marked “inferred from the sample variables”). The machine-written design statement is shown only when the two readings agreed on the design type, and every statement was then checked against its source text, which removed details the text does not state. The groups are the per-sample intervention arms when arms are resolved, then the study's own comparison_group (a sample attribute or name stem whose value → group map both readings agreed on and an automated check against the text confirmed value by value), otherwise the per-sample health_condition values held by at least 3 samples and 1 % of the project. The matrix counts people (distinct subject_id) per group and body_site_code, or samples when fewer than half the samples name a person; control samples are left out. The table under it names, for each design element, the field and the attributes or texts its values were read from, with their share. The checks compare the paper (stated participants, repeated sampling, compared groups, trial arms, related people) with what the sample variables show; a warning means the deposit does not let you reproduce that part of the design, not that the paper is wrong.

Self-contradictory metadata (red ! on the Projects table and project pages). When two sample-level sources disagree about the same sample, neither value is published — see Methods § 7.

Who and when: who each sample came from and at which time point (228 projects, 8,724 person identifiers and 21,879 time-point labels). For projects whose source describes repeated sampling but whose samples could not be assigned to people, or whose repeatedly sampled people had no time axis, the catalog lists the sample variables that could say who and when: attributes whose values repeat like person identifiers, and sample names split into a person part and a time part (for example P01_D7 → person P01, time D7; the split is kept only when the person part repeats, each person-and-time pair is unique and the time part varies within persons). Two independent model readings of the project's text choose the person variable and the time variable and give, for every time value, its label and the days since the time origin, only when the text or the value states them. The person variable is kept when both readings choose it, both say the prose describes repeated sampling, its number of people fits the stated number of participants and sex does not change within a person; time values are kept when both readings give the same days (or the same label). An automated check against the text then tests the person variable against example samples and every time value against the text; only what it confirms is written, as a value from the archive record with its own time-point label and day offset. Projects where it found wrong time points were withheld, together with two error patterns checked across all outputs (an age used as a time axis for people sampled once; a code such as "W-2", two weeks before, merged with "W2").

Resolved vs. not resolved. A longitudinal project is resolved (solid sheet tag L, badge "longitudinal") when its repeatedly sampled people have samples placed on a time axis, so the gap between samples is known and the per-person timeline can be drawn; otherwise it is not resolved (dotted L, badge "longitudinal — not resolved"): the source describes repeated sampling but the samples lack ages, full dates or convertible timepoint labels, or cannot be assigned to people. Relatedness is resolved (solid R) when related or co-located people are linked to their samples — families or shared settings with at least two catalogued people, or explicit pairwise relations such as FMT donor → recipient — so the relationship graphs can be drawn; a dotted R means the paper describes them but the link to samples is not resolved yet. An intervention project (randomised or non-randomised controlled, crossover, single-arm before/after, or observational with a procedure) gets a solid I when at least 80 % of its samples carry a per-sample arm (the intervention column) — the project page then draws the arms by design — and a dotted I otherwise.

Relatedness and shared settings (374 projects; 5,930 families, 3,985 of them with a sampled parent and child; 610 shared settings). Four fields record it per sample: family_id (the source's family, dyad, household or twin-pair id), family_role (mother, father, parent, child, twin, sibling, partner, grandparent, other relative, household member), and shared_setting_type / shared_setting_id (a household, village, tribe, daycare or school, care home, ward, military unit, team or workplace that at least two sampled people share — never the whole cohort or the city of recruitment). They come from archive attributes, sample-name tokens whose meaning the source states, supplementary tables and paper text, each with its quote; relatedness is never inferred from similar ages or a shared country. Families are summarised as structures (e.g. "mother + child×2"), drawn as small diagrams on the project page, and listed in catalog_relatedness.parquet (one row per family or shared setting); relations that roles cannot express — twin zygosity, sibling pairs, FMT donor → recipient — are in catalog_relations.parquet. The largest kinds are mother and infant 105, FMT donor and recipient 95, households 58, families 43, twins 40; the Sampling filter on Projects has the rest. The Projects table has a Sampling filter that selects longitudinal projects and each kind of relatedness; tags L and R mark them in the table.
